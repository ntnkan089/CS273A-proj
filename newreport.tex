\begin{abstract}
We looked at the 30-day hospital readmission prediction among diabetic patients using the Diabetes 130--US Hospitals dataset. 
The data presented noisy categorical variables, irregular missingness, and large variance amongst clinical profiles. 
After exploratory analysis and data preprocessing, we train and compare Logistic Regression, Random Forests, Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs). 
The focus of this report is not only on final performance but also on model selection, parameter choices, and diagnosing under-fitting versus over-fitting using cross-validation and complexity curves. 
On a split test set, a tuned Random Forest reaches ROC--AUC $\approx 0.69$ and F1 $\approx 0.62$, with a tuned MLP close behind.
We discuss how dataset characteristics motivated our feature design and how our tuning decisions were driven by validation performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Unplanned hospital readmissions are costly and often indicate poor transitions from hostpital care to home care.
Patients with diabetes are high risk because they tend to have multiple medical conditions, medication, and visits with the healthcare system.
Accurate prediction of readmission risk can help improve follow-up resources and improve patients lives.

The goal is to build and compare several machine learning models for predicting whether a hospitalized diabetic patient will be readmitted **within 30 days** after discharge.
This task serves as a case study in feature design, model selection, and complexity control. 

We first perform exploratory analysis to understand missingness patterns, outcome balance, and basic relationships between demographics and the other variables. We use these insights to guide preprocessing.
Next, we explore techniques less focused on in class such as Random Forests, SVMs with RBF kernels, and Neural Networks where we tune their hyperparameters.
After, we apply stratified cross-validation for parameter selection and inspect complexity curves to examine under-fitting and over-fitting.
Finally, we evaluate all models on a separate test set and compare accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and ROC--AUC.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}

\subsection{Dataset and target definition}

The Diabetes 130--US Hospitals dataset contains 101{,}766 encounters and about 50 variables, including demographics, ICD-9 diagnosis codes, lab results, procedures, medication use, and admission/discharge information.
The original outcome variable \texttt{readmitted} has three levels: ``NO'', ``$<30$'' (readmitted within 30 days), and ``$>30$'' (readmitted at some later time).

For this project we define a binary target for readmission
\[
y =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if readmitted $<30$ or $>30$},\\
0 & \text{if NO readmission is recorded.}
\end{cases}
\]
Under this definition the cleaned dataset has 99{,}340 encounters with about 52.9\% non-readmitted and 47.1\% readmitted, which is substantially more balanced than the original three-class formulation.

\subsection{Missingness and feature design}

Several variables use ``?'' as a missing-value code.
Figure~\ref{fig:missing} (left) shows that \texttt{weight}, \texttt{payer\_code}, and \texttt{medical\_specialty} are missing in 40--98\% of encounters.
These columns were dropped because their sparsity made them difficult to model reliably.
Diagnosis columns and key utilization variables had much lower missingness and were retained.

ICD-9 diagnosis codes are extremely high-dimensional and sparse.
We mapped the three diagnosis fields (\texttt{diag\_1--3}) into 8 broad groups (circulatory, respiratory, digestive, endocrine/diabetes, injury, musculoskeletal, genitourinary, neoplasms, and other) using simple ranges on the numeric codes.
This grouping reduces dimensionality while preserving broad clinical patterns.

Twenty-one diabetes-related medications had four levels (``No'', ``Steady'', ``Up'', ``Down'').
We collapsed these into binary indicators for ``any use or adjustment'' versus ``no use''.
This decision was based on both sparsity and the observation that any non-zero level roughly indicates active management.

Hospital admission and discharge codes were grouped into a smaller set of clinically similar categories and then one-hot encoded.
Gender, diabetes medication flag (\texttt{diabetesMed}), and medication change flag (\texttt{change}) were coded as 0/1.
Age bands were mapped to integer codes 0--9.

After preprocessing, the final feature matrix contained 60 columns, including grouped diagnoses, one-hot encoded categorical variables, medication indicators, and utilization counts such as number of inpatient visits, procedures, and medications.

\subsection{Train--test split and balancing}

We used an 80/20 stratified split into train and test sets.
To improve model training, we addressed class imbalance only in the training set: the majority class (not readmitted) was downsampled to match the minority class, producing a balanced training sample of 37{,}453 readmitted and 37{,}453 non-readmitted encounters.
The test set retained the original 53/47 prevalence so that evaluation reflects a realistic clinical distribution.

A \texttt{StandardScaler} was fitted on the balanced training features and then applied to both training and test sets for models sensitive to feature scaling (Logistic Regression, SVM, and MLP).
Tree-based models (Decision Trees and Random Forests) used the unscaled features.

\section{Models and Hyperparameter Selection}

We implemented four main model families:
Logistic Regression, Random Forests, SVMs with RBF kernel, and MLP Neural Networks.
Our emphasis was on how we chose hyperparameters and controlled complexity, rather than on exhaustive grid search.

Stratified $k$-fold cross-validation on the balanced training data was used throughout for model comparison and tuning, with ROC--AUC as the primary score.

\subsection{Logistic Regression}

Logistic Regression serves as a linear baseline and a sanity check for the more complex models.
We used:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Solver: \texttt{lbfgs}
  \item Class weights: \texttt{balanced}
  \item Maximum iterations: 300
\end{itemize}
This configuration requires little tuning and provides interpretable coefficients.
It also gives a point of comparison for how much benefit the nonlinear models provide.

\subsection{Random Forests}

Random Forests are well-suited to this dataset because they:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Handle a mix of continuous and categorical (one-hot) variables.
  \item Capture nonlinear interactions between features such as diagnoses, prior utilization, and medication use.
  \item Are relatively robust to noisy predictors and outliers.
\end{itemize}

Rather than running a large automatic grid search, we manually explored a small set of parameter combinations based on bias--variance considerations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{n\_estimators} $\in \{100, 200\}$
  \item \texttt{max\_depth} $\in \{10, 20, \text{None}\}$
  \item \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $\in \{1, 5\}$
\end{itemize}
For each configuration we computed 5-fold stratified CV ROC--AUC.

Depth values larger than 20 gave training accuracy close to 1.0 but did not improve validation AUC, indicating overfitting.
Increasing \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} from 1 to 5 slightly reduced training performance but improved validation performance and stabilized feature importance.

The best model according to cross-validation was:
\[
\texttt{n\_estimators} = 200,\quad
\texttt{max\_depth} = 20,\quad
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} = 5,
\]
with mean CV ROC--AUC $\approx 0.685$.
We refer to this as the ``tuned'' Random Forest.

\subsection{Support Vector Machine (RBF)}

We considered SVMs with RBF kernel as a flexible, margin-based nonlinear model.
However, training RBF SVMs on tens of thousands of samples is computationally expensive ($\mathcal{O}(n^2)$).
To keep training feasible we:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Subsampled 20{,}000 examples from the balanced training set.
  \item Used \texttt{C = 1.0} as a moderate regularization strength; higher $C$ values led to increased variance without clear gains.
  \item Used \texttt{gamma = ``scale''}, which adapts the kernel width to the feature dimensionality.
  \item Set \texttt{class\_weight = ``balanced''}.
\end{itemize}
Under these settings, SVM performance on the test set was comparable to Logistic Regression but did not surpass the tuned Random Forest or MLP.

\subsection{Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)}

The MLP is the most flexible model considered here and required careful complexity control.
The dataset has only moderate signal-to-noise ratio and relatively simple structure, so very deep networks risk severe overfitting.

We varied three aspects of the architecture and optimization:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hidden layer structures: $(32)$, $(64)$, $(128)$, and $(128, 64)$.
  \item L2 regularization strength $\alpha \in \{10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}\}$.
  \item Initial learning rate $\in \{10^{-3}, 5\times 10^{-4}\}$.
\end{itemize}
Other settings were fixed: ReLU activations, batch size 256, maximum 50 epochs, and early stopping based on validation loss.

Figure~\ref{fig:complex} (right) shows a complexity curve where we vary only the hidden layer size and plot mean training and validation accuracy across CV folds.
Training accuracy steadily increases with network size, while validation accuracy is nearly flat and drops for the $(128, 64)$ architecture, indicating mild overfitting.

The chosen configuration balances expressiveness with generalization:
a single hidden layer with 128 units, $\alpha=0.001$, and learning rate $5\times 10^{-4}$.
We refer to this as the ``tuned'' MLP.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

This project illustrates a full machine learning workflow on a real clinical dataset, with an emphasis on how model and parameter choices are guided by the data and validation results.

Exploratory analysis revealed heavy missingness in some variables, motivating their removal, and highlighted the importance of utilization measures and diagnosis groups.
Feature engineering focused on grouping ICD-9 codes, binarizing medication use, and encoding hospital identifiers.
Balancing the training set by downsampling the majority class, while leaving the test set unchanged, produced models with similar precision and recall for the positive class.

Random Forests and MLPs offered the best performance, with Random Forests slightly ahead in ROC--AUC and F1, and MLPs slightly ahead in recall.
Complexity curves showed classic under- and over-fitting patterns:
deep single trees overfit badly, whereas moderate-depth ensembles and modestly sized neural networks generalize better.
SVMs with RBF kernel were competitive but did not provide clear gains over simpler models, possibly due to computational constraints and noisy features.

Several lessons emerge:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Dataset structure should guide preprocessing; clever feature design is at least as important as sophisticated modeling.
  \item Hyperparameter tuning is best done iteratively with cross-validation and diagnostic plots, rather than blindly via large grids.
  \item Evaluating both discrimination (ROC--AUC) and error trade-offs (precision, recall, F1) is essential, especially when false negatives have clinical cost.
\end{itemize}

Future work could incorporate cost-sensitive learning, probability calibration, or temporal models that exploit multiple encounters per patient.
Even modest improvements in prediction could help hospitals better allocate follow-up resources and reduce avoidable readmissions.
